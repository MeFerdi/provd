On **2026-04-18 at 17:35:35 UTC** (Ethereum block 24,908,285) an attacker delivered a forged LayerZero message from Unichain to Kelp's rsETH OFT adapter on Ethereum and released **116,500 rsETH (~$292M)**.

Public accounts of the root cause disagreed. One analysis said the adapter was *deployed* with a single verifier; another said it had been *downgraded* from 2-of-2 to 1-of-1. We rebuilt the adapter's configuration history from public on-chain data to see which was right.

## Summary

- The exploited route (Unichain, eid 30320 → Ethereum) accepted messages attested by **one required verifier — the LayerZero Labs DVN — and no optional verifiers**.
- That setting was made on **2025-04-02 08:14 UTC**, **381 days before the exploit**, and was not changed again before it.
- At the time of the exploit, **24 of the adapter's 26** receive routes were single-verifier.
- The Unichain setting was made by a **single private key (EOA)** that held the adapter's LayerZero delegate role — the account that can change its verifier configuration — from 2025-01-02 to 2025-08-25. It was not the protocol's multisig.
- The widely reported **2-of-2 → 1-of-1 downgrade was real, but it happened on 2024-03-21 — 758 days before the exploit** — and on the Blast and Mode routes, not on Unichain.

Both public accounts were partly right. There was a downgrade, two years earlier; the exploited route itself was never configured with more than one verifier.

Every one of these settings was public and readable on-chain the whole time. A monitor reading them would have flagged the exploited route the day it was opened.

## Who could change the configuration

The account that can change an OApp's verifier configuration on the LayerZero endpoint is its *delegate*. For Kelp's adapter that role was held by:

| From | To | Account | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024-03-15 | 2025-01-02 | `0x1f7A…769b` | EOA (deployer key) |
| 2025-01-02 | 2025-08-25 | `0xCF7b…5662` | EOA |
| 2025-08-25 | — | `0xCbcd…29A1` | Safe, 3-of-6 |

For roughly 20 months the adapter's verifier configuration could be changed by a single private key rather than the protocol's multisig. This point was not covered in the public reporting we reviewed. By the time of the exploit the delegate was the 3-of-6 Safe; the single-key period is a separate configuration risk, and it was also readable on-chain.

## What a configuration monitor would have shown

Under the [Provd methodology](/methodology), the Unichain route matches `LZ-SINGLE-VERIFIER` (critical) from 2025-04-02 onwards, and the delegate account matches `ADM-EOA-DELEGATE` (critical) until 2025-08-25. A change monitor would also have raised `CHG-NEW-ROUTE`, escalated to critical, on the day the Unichain route was configured as single-verifier.

That is a statement about visibility, not prevention. A flag does not stop a forged message; it gives the protocol team, its curators and its insurers the information to act on before one arrives.

## Method

1. Decode the exploit transaction's `PacketDelivered` event to get the source endpoint (eid 30320) and the receiving OApp, `0x85d4…8ef3` (`RSETH_OFTAdapter`).
2. Read `EndpointV2.delegates(oapp)` and the adapter's `setDelegate` history to find the three delegates over time.
3. Decode every `EndpointV2.setConfig` call made by each delegate (Blockscout for the EOAs, the Safe Transaction Service for the Safe) and replay the ULN (config type 2) changes in time order.
4. Cross-check: the current `getConfig` for eid 30320 matches the Safe's 2026-04-23 configuration.
5. Resolve DVN names with LayerZero's public metadata registry.

## Limitation

Free RPC endpoints do not serve historical state, so the configuration at each point in time is reconstructed from configuration transactions rather than read directly with a historical `eth_call`. An archive node can confirm each point directly.
